How to Order a Magician: The Complete Guide

A practical checklist for deciding whether — and how — to book a magician, whether you're planning a family simcha or a corporate event.

By Yishai Rasowsky  ·  August 2026  ·  10 min read  ·  Print / save as PDF

Every week I talk to people trying to decide whether hiring a magician is even the right call for their event — and if it is, how to actually go about it. The questions are almost always the same, whether it's a mother planning her son's bar mitzvah or an HR manager planning a company dinner. So I put together this guide: one version for family events, one for corporate events, and a shared checklist underneath both.

Use it as a rulebook. Print it, forward it to your co-planner, or just skim the checklists before you make any calls.

The short version

Should you book a magician at all? If your event has a lull — a cocktail hour, a wait for food, a stretch where people are just standing around — yes. That's exactly where a magician earns their fee.

What matters most? Not the tricks. It's whether the performer can read your specific room and adapt to it. Ask about that before you ask about pricing.

How far in advance? 4–8 weeks for a normal date. 3+ months if your event falls near Chanukah, Purim, or the summer bein hazmanim season, when good performers book up fast.

Is a Magician Actually the Right Call?

Before any checklist, it's worth being honest about the tradeoffs — because "everyone books a magician" isn't a good enough reason, and I'd rather you go in with clear eyes than feel talked into it.

The concern

A magician usually costs more per hour than a DJ, a photo booth, or background music.

The reframe

True, dollar for dollar. But a DJ or photo booth is broadcast entertainment — it plays whether or not anyone's paying attention. A magician spends nearly the entire booking in direct, one-on-one contact with your actual guests. You're not paying to fill silence; you're paying for guaranteed personal attention to every person in the room.

The concern

Isn't it just a novelty — fun for five minutes, then people move on?

The reframe

The opposite tends to happen. A magic moment doesn't end when the trick ends — it's the thing two strangers immediately turn to each other and start talking about. That reaction is the real product: a built-in conversation starter, on demand, for a room full of people who might not otherwise have a reason to speak to each other.

The concern

We already have music or a band — isn't that enough entertainment?

The reframe

Music fills a room; it doesn't introduce people to each other. Close-up magic works the opposite way — moving table to table, drawing three or four strangers into one shared moment of amazement. At a corporate event, that's a built-in icebreaker between people standing at opposite ends of the room. At a wedding or simcha, it's what actually gets a table of distant cousins or unfamiliar guests talking to each other.

What you're really paying for

  • Guaranteed one-on-one attention for every guest — not just the ones near the stage or dance floor.
  • A built-in icebreaker that gets strangers talking, mingling, and networking without anyone having to force it.
  • Zero dead time. While a band plays to whoever's already dancing, close-up magic goes and fills the lulls — cocktail hour, the wait between courses, the awkward first twenty minutes before people loosen up.
  • A story people repeat. Live, interactive, impossible-seeming moments are what guests are still telling each other about days later — which also means they're describing your event to people who weren't there.
  • Flexibility. The same performer scales from a table of four to a ballroom of four hundred, and adjusts in real time to the room's mood.

Quick gut check

A magician is probably a strong fit if any of these are true for your event:

  • Guests won't all know each other — a multi-family simcha, a corporate mixer, a conference
  • There's a stretch of time with nothing programmed — cocktail hour, before the ceremony, between courses
  • You want people still talking about it a week later, not just a nice backdrop for photos
  • You'd rather budget for an experience than a decoration

If that sounds like your event, the rest of this guide is about making sure you book the right one.

👨‍👩‍👧‍👦  For Family Events & Simchas

Bar mitzvahs, bat mitzvahs, sheva berachos, birthdays, Chanukah parties, brissim — these events share one thing: a mixed-age crowd that includes both a 6-year-old and a grandparent, often in the same room, at the same time.

What to look for

  • Comfort with mixed ages. A show built only for kids loses the adults in the first five minutes. A show built only for adults loses the kids. You want someone who moves between both.
  • Bilingual fluency. Most Israeli simchas mix Hebrew and English speakers. A performer who can switch naturally — not just recite a translated script — keeps everyone in the moment together.
  • Ability to make the guest of honor the star. At a bar mitzvah or birthday, the best magicians find a way to spotlight the person being celebrated without embarrassing them.
  • A plan for the room's actual layout. Living room, hall, restaurant back room — ask how they'll work the specific space, not a generic answer.
  • Warmth over polish. At a family event, guests remember how the performer made them feel more than how clean the trick was.

Questions to ask

  • Have you performed at this type of simcha before? A bar mitzvah, a sheva berachos, and a bris all have a different rhythm — you want someone who already knows the shape of your event, not someone learning on your dime.
  • What do you do when the kids and adults are in the same room? Listen for a real answer, not "I keep everyone entertained."
  • Can you work close-up (tableside) as well as a stage segment? Many family events benefit from both — close-up during the meal, a short stage moment for the whole room.
  • What's your policy if the event runs late or the schedule shifts? Simchas rarely run exactly on time. You want flexibility built in.

Red flags for family events

  • Can't answer how they'd handle a room with kids ages 4 to 14 in it at once
  • No willingness to do a short site check-in call before the event
  • Vague or evasive when you ask for recent references from similar simchas
  • Only has kids'-party material — no material that also works for the adults in the room
💼  For Corporate Events

Corporate audiences are a different animal — and, honestly, some of the most satisfying to perform for. They've seen professional presenters before, they're often a little guarded at the start, and that makes it more rewarding when a room full of executives ends up completely hooked.

What to look for

  • Polish and pacing. Corporate crowds notice production quality — timing, transitions, how the performer handles a microphone and a room's sightlines.
  • Ability to work without alcohol-fueled energy. Corporate rooms are quieter and more reserved than a wedding dance floor. A performer needs to build energy from a cold start, not just ride an already-loud room.
  • Discretion with sensitive material. No off-color humor, no material that could embarrass an employee or a client in front of colleagues.
  • Ability to fold in your company or message. Not required, but a strong bonus — a mentalism segment that references your brand, a product name, or a company milestone lands memorably without feeling like a forced plug.
  • Logistics readiness. AV requirements, whether they need a table or stage, how much space they need, whether they can do a walk-around cocktail-hour segment versus a seated stage show.
  • Virtual/hybrid capability, if relevant. If part of your team is remote, ask whether the performer has run a show that works for a mixed in-room and Zoom audience.

Questions to ask

  • Have you performed for a corporate audience of our size and format before? A 20-person dinner and a 300-person conference floor require different skill sets.
  • Can the material be tailored to reference our company or industry? Ask for an example of how they've done this before.
  • What are your AV and space requirements? Get this in writing early so your venue and event planner can plan around it.
  • Do you carry your own insurance / can you provide documentation if our venue requires it? Many corporate venues and hotels require proof of liability coverage — ask early, not the week of.
  • What's the cancellation and rescheduling policy? Corporate dates shift more than family dates. Know the terms before you sign anything.

Red flags for corporate events

  • No written quote, contract, or confirmation of date/time/location before you pay a deposit
  • Can't describe how the show would open with a quiet, reserved room
  • No references from prior corporate (as opposed to only family/party) bookings
  • Unwilling to do a short pre-event call with your event coordinator or HR contact

The Universal Checklist (Any Event)

Whatever kind of event you're planning, these apply across the board:

  • Book 4–8 weeks out for a standard date; 3+ months out for peak seasons (Chanukah, Purim, summer bein hazmanim).
  • Get everything in writing — date, time, location, duration, price, and what's included.
  • Confirm the format — close-up/tableside, stage show, walk-around, or a mix — matches your venue and guest flow.
  • Ask about a deposit and cancellation policy before you commit.
  • Watch real footage — actual crowd reactions from real events, not just a polished highlight reel.
  • Confirm language(s) if your guests are a Hebrew/English mix, or if you need a fully English or fully Hebrew show.
  • Ask what happens if a guest tries to expose or heckle — the answer tells you how they'll handle pressure in the room.

One Last Thing

The goal of any of this isn't to find the performer with the flashiest trick list. It's to find someone who will read your specific room — your specific mix of people, your specific event — and adapt to it in real time. That's the difference between polite applause and a room that's still talking about it on the way home.
