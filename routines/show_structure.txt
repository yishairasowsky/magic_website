SHOW STRUCTURE — YISHAI
=======================
Based on answers in show_structure_questions.txt


BEN COHEN'S NOTES  [apply everywhere]
--------------------------------------

1. NEVER ASK FOR APPLAUSE.
   Not "give a round of applause for [NAME]." Not "let's hear it for her." Nothing.
   If the moment earns applause, the room will give it. If it doesn't, asking makes it worse.
   You were obsessed with it through the entire show, not just the opening.
   Forcing applause doesn't make a bored audience less bored.

   Transition fix: say something directly to the spectator that signals they're done
   ("thank you [NAME]") and turn away. The audience takes the cue.

2. STOP OVER-PROVING.
   "I have never touched this book." "That bill has been in your pocket all night."
   "I printed this poster weeks ago." Every time you say it, you make it sound less true.
   State the facts once, plainly, at the start. Then never repeat them.
   The audience heard you the first time. Trust them.

3. USE FEWER WORDS.
   The first 8 minutes should have been 3. Every routine takes longer than it needs to.
   The fewer words you use to get an idea across, the better the effect lands.
   Cut anything that isn't earning its time on stage.

4. REMOVE THE OPENING JOKE.

5. TAKE CREDIT FOR THE AMAZEMENT.
   Don't just let the amazement happen -- that's describing a coincidence that has nothing
   to do with you. The audience has no reason to react to you specifically.
   Your ability has to be the clear cause of what they just witnessed.
   You like putting spectators as the stars, but in a stand-alone show you have to take
   ownership of the moment or it doesn't land as your effect.

6. REVELATIONS MUST BE INSTANTLY APPARENT TO EVERYONE AT ONCE.
   No multi-step confirmations. No "look at the number, now look at the paper, now ask her
   if that's right." By the time the last person figures it out, the moment is dead.
   Design every reveal so the entire room gets it simultaneously, without needing to
   be walked through it.

   Phone unlock example: if you have her PIN, don't show switched numbers in a child's hand
   that nobody can see. Open the phone with it. Everyone in the room immediately understands
   what just happened -- no explanation needed.

7. DO THE MATERIAL YOUR AUDIENCE WANTS TO SEE.
   If you do something they didn't ask for, you have to oversell why it's amazing
   before you do it -- otherwise they have no framework for caring.

8. EVERY ROUTINE NEEDS A PREMISE BEFORE IT STARTS.
   Right now all your effects are just describing actions -- "I'm going to flip through
   the pages, you say stop..." Nobody cares yet. You haven't given them a reason to.
   Before every routine: state a phenomenon. Something people claim is true or isn't.
   Frame it so the audience is curious before a single action happens.
   You have under one minute to do this. If you can't make it compelling in under a minute,
   cut it down until you can. By the last sentence of your setup, the room should be leaning in.

   The premise can be as unbelievable as possible -- you're presenting it as something
   true or not, for the audience to decide. You're not proving anything. You're demonstrating.

9. YOU ARE NOT WRITING A PREDICTION. YOU ARE SENSING SOMETHING.
   "I wrote this before tonight" = prediction frame. Cold. Mechanical. A trick.
   "I wrote down what I was sensing" = reading frame. Personal. Alive. Your ability.
   This is the difference between a puzzle and a performance.
   Every reveal should be framed as something you felt or sensed -- not something you prepared.

   Practical note: Pre-fold the papers for names and place (2BT) before the show.


THE PREMISE
-----------

You are not presenting yourself as a supernatural figure.
You are the most entertaining thing that will happen to this room tonight.

The show is not "a mentalist reads minds."
The show is "impossible things are going to happen, and we are going to have a great time."

The guest of honor is the thread. Every show has one -- the bar mitzvah boy, the anniversary
couple, the birthday person. They are the star. You are there to make them look good.
Introduce this idea at the top and return to it at the end.


BEN COHEN -- PER ROUTINE NOTES  [from watching the full show video]
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The show ran 40 minutes for 6 effects. It should be 30 or less.
Ben watched it straight through and felt what the audience felt.

WHITE DWARF (opener):
  Ran 8 minutes. Must be 3 minutes max. The opener has one job: get in fast,
  land hard, get out. Everything else in the show depends on this first impression.

BOOK TEST:
  Too many people on stage. Don't push or manhandle the audience getting people up.
  Fewer bodies, cleaner staging.

PK TOUCH:
  Way too much setup time before anything happens. Cut the preamble drastically.
  The effect itself got a good reaction -- the problem is everything before it.
  Note: anyone sitting at a bad angle may not have been able to see the taps.
  Consider your sightlines before positioning the spectator.

NAMES AND PLACE (2BT):
  Restrict the choices so you can prepare strong pre-canned lines in advance.
  Example: woman thinks of a female name, man thinks of a male name.
  Or restrict to a category -- sports star, singer -- something with comic potential.
  Pre-fold the papers before the show.

CELL PHONE UNLOCK:
  Works better as an interruption mid-routine than as a standalone.
  The reveal must be visible to the whole room at once -- turn the phone
  toward the audience on the final number so everyone sees it open simultaneously.
  If the audience doesn't see the phone open, the effect fails completely.

CRYPTEXT:
  Had no meaning, no grounding, no purpose. You cut the long setup process but
  accidentally cut the context with it -- so nobody knew why to care.
  The audience had already checked out as a group by this point.
  Needs a full premise rewrite. The context is not optional.

GENERAL -- STAGING:
  Be mindful of keeping spectators on stage for too long.
  The longer someone stands up there, the more pressure it puts on you to perform
  and the more uncomfortable it gets for them and the audience.
  Get people up, use them, get them seated.


MAGICIAN IN TROUBLE
--------------------

This is gold and you should build it into every routine where it's possible.
The structure is: audience thinks you got it wrong, then you get it exactly right.

Book test version: Don't just claim the word. Make them check. Say "open the book,
find the page, find the line, find the word -- and tell me if I'm close, in the neighborhood,
or exactly right." The suspense of them checking is the trick.

Animal version (Krauss): Write Newton, cross it out, write Einstein. Then ask "did you
think of Newton first?" Almost always yes. That's two hits for the price of one.

Rule: Never rush to your own defense. Let them think you're wrong for a moment.
The longer that tension holds, the bigger the release.


SEQUENCE LOGIC
--------------

Current problem: the routines are ordered by convenience, not by emotional arc.

Suggested arc:

  INTRO
  -- establish the guest of honor as the throughline --

  1. KRAUSS OPENER (modified -- no mandatory standing, raise hands instead)
     Opens with the whole room. Gets everyone involved immediately.
     Energy is high, funny, group participation.

  2. NAMES AND PLACE (2BT)
     First intimate routine. Two people, two private thoughts.
     Drops the energy slightly, makes it personal. Good contrast after the opener.

  3. MAGIC SQUARE
     Brings energy back up. Visual, impressive, involves one clear spectator.
     Good "wow" moment that doesn't require belief in mind reading.

  4. ULTIMATE VACATION + CLOCK
     Playful and funny. Lots of audience participation in choosing the details.
     This is where you get the laughs -- the premise is inherently comic.

  5. BOOK TEST  [peak effect]
     Your strongest routine goes here -- not at the end, but at the top of the second half.
     By this point the audience trusts you and is fully invested.
     Use the Magician in Trouble structure. Let them check the book themselves.

  6. PK TOUCH
     Quiet and intimate after the peak. This is the "breath" of the show.
     Reframe it before you start: explain clearly why what they're about to see is impossible.
     This routine works best when the audience is leaning in, not fired up.

  7. WHITE DWARF  [to be scripted]
     Rebuilds energy going into the closer.

  8. CRYPTEXT  [closer]
     The big prepared prediction. Fix the setup (see above) and fix the last line.


TRANSITIONS
-----------

No dead time. Every transition has three parts:

  a) SEND OFF the spectator -- no applause cue.
     Say something directly to them ("thank you [NAME]") and turn away.
     If the room applauds, great. Don't ask for it.

  b) RESET physically -- get what you need from your table -- but keep talking.
     Never turn your back on the audience in silence.

  c) BRIDGE LINE into the next routine -- no announcement, just flow.
     Don't say "and now for my next trick." Just start talking about the next idea
     as if it just occurred to you.

Example transition from Book Test into PK Touch:

  "Thank you [NAME]. [she sits]
   Most of what I do involves information -- words, numbers, names, places.
   But there's one thing I've always been curious about. Whether I can affect something
   that has nothing to do with information at all. Something purely physical.
   I need someone brave for this..."

The bridge line connects the two routines thematically. It makes the show feel like
one continuous idea, not a list.


THE LAST LINE OF THE SHOW
--------------------------

"Thank you, you have been a wonderful audience" is a curtain call, not a closer.

The last line should be the last beat of Cryptext -- after they've seen the serial number
on the poster. You want one sentence that lands like a period at the end of the whole evening.

Something like:
  "That number has been waiting in that poster since before any of you walked through that door."

Then silence. Then bow. Then the room decides what to do next -- and they will.


GUEST OF HONOR THROUGHLINE
---------------------------

Introduce early: "Tonight is [NAME]'s evening. Everything that happens here tonight --
I want it to be for [NAME]."

Involve them in at least one routine -- ideally the book test or ultimate vacation,
where their choice drives the effect.

Return at the end: the last moment before the bow should reference them by name.
"[NAME] -- I hope tonight was everything you wanted it to be."

This makes the whole show feel like it was designed specifically for this event,
not just performed at it.
