BEN COHEN'S NOTES  [apply everywhere]
--------------------------------------

1. NEVER ASK FOR APPLAUSE.
   Not "give a round of applause for [NAME]." Not "let's hear it for her." Nothing.
   If the moment earns applause, the room will give it. If it doesn't, asking makes it worse.
   Cut every directed-applause line from every routine.

   Transition fix: instead of "give it up for [NAME]," just say something to [NAME] directly
   that signals they're done -- "thank you [NAME]" and turn away. The audience takes the cue.

2. STOP OVER-PROVING.
   "I have never touched this book." "That bill has been in your pocket all night."
   "I printed this poster weeks ago." Every time you say it, you make it sound less true.
   State the facts once, plainly, at the start. Then never repeat them.
   The audience heard you the first time. Trust them.


THE PREMISE
-----------

You are not presenting yourself as a supernatural figure.
You are the most entertaining thing that will happen to this room tonight.

The show is not "a mentalist reads minds."
The show is "impossible things are going to happen, and we are going to have a great time."

The guest of honor is the thread. Every show has one -- the bar mitzvah boy, the anniversary
couple, the birthday person. They are the star. You are there to make them look good.
Introduce this idea at the top and return to it at the end.


WHY PK TOUCH AND CRYPTEXT AREN'T LANDING
-----------------------------------------

You said these two didn't get a great reaction in your last show. Here's why that probably is:

PK TOUCH: It's a quiet, intimate routine. One person on stage, eyes closed, small subtle
moments. If it comes after something big and loud, the room deflates. The problem is probably
placement, not the trick. It also needs a clearer setup -- the audience has to understand
WHY this is impossible before it starts, not just after it ends.

CRYPTEXT: The calculator force can feel like a math trick rather than a miracle. The audience
doesn't always track the logic of why the number on the bill and the number on the poster
are connected. If they don't follow the chain, the reveal lands flat. The fix is in the setup --
you need to clearly establish all three objects (poster, bill, calculator number) as independent
BEFORE connecting them. Also: "thank you, you have been a wonderful audience" is a weak
button for a closer. The last line of the show needs to hit harder.


MAGICIAN IN TROUBLE
--------------------

This is gold and you should build it into every routine where it's possible.
The structure is: audience thinks you got it wrong, then you get it exactly right.

Book test version: Don't just claim the word. Make them check. Say "open the book,
find the page, find the line, find the word -- and tell me if I'm close, in the neighborhood,
or exactly right." The suspense of them checking is the trick.

Animal version (Krauss): Write Newton, cross it out, write Einstein. Then ask "did you
think of Newton first?" Almost always yes. That's two hits for the price of one.

Rule: Never rush to your own defense. Let them think you're wrong for a moment.
The longer that tension holds, the bigger the release.


SEQUENCE LOGIC
--------------

Current problem: the routines are ordered by convenience, not by emotional arc.

Suggested arc:

  INTRO
  -- establish the guest of honor as the throughline --

  1. KRAUSS OPENER (modified -- no mandatory standing, raise hands instead)
     Opens with the whole room. Gets everyone involved immediately.
     Energy is high, funny, group participation.

  2. NAMES AND PLACE (2BT)
     First intimate routine. Two people, two private thoughts.
     Drops the energy slightly, makes it personal. Good contrast after the opener.

  3. MAGIC SQUARE
     Brings energy back up. Visual, impressive, involves one clear spectator.
     Good "wow" moment that doesn't require belief in mind reading.

  4. ULTIMATE VACATION + CLOCK
     Playful and funny. Lots of audience participation in choosing the details.
     This is where you get the laughs -- the premise is inherently comic.

  5. BOOK TEST  [peak effect]
     Your strongest routine goes here -- not at the end, but at the top of the second half.
     By this point the audience trusts you and is fully invested.
     Use the Magician in Trouble structure. Let them check the book themselves.

  6. PK TOUCH
     Quiet and intimate after the peak. This is the "breath" of the show.
     Reframe it before you start: explain clearly why what they're about to see is impossible.
     This routine works best when the audience is leaning in, not fired up.

  7. WHITE DWARF  [to be scripted]
     Rebuilds energy going into the closer.

  8. CRYPTEXT  [closer]
     The big prepared prediction. Fix the setup (see above) and fix the last line.


TRANSITIONS
-----------

No dead time. Every transition has three parts:

  a) SEND OFF the spectator -- no applause cue.
     Say something directly to them ("thank you [NAME]") and turn away.
     If the room applauds, great. Don't ask for it.

  b) RESET physically -- get what you need from your table -- but keep talking.
     Never turn your back on the audience in silence.

  c) BRIDGE LINE into the next routine -- no announcement, just flow.
     Don't say "and now for my next trick." Just start talking about the next idea
     as if it just occurred to you.

Example transition from Book Test into PK Touch:

  "Thank you [NAME]. [she sits]
   Most of what I do involves information -- words, numbers, names, places.
   But there's one thing I've always been curious about. Whether I can affect something
   that has nothing to do with information at all. Something purely physical.
   I need someone brave for this..."

The bridge line connects the two routines thematically. It makes the show feel like
one continuous idea, not a list.


THE LAST LINE OF THE SHOW
--------------------------

"Thank you, you have been a wonderful audience" is a curtain call, not a closer.

The last line should be the last beat of Cryptext -- after they've seen the serial number
on the poster. You want one sentence that lands like a period at the end of the whole evening.

Something like:
  "That number has been waiting in that poster since before any of you walked through that door."

Then silence. Then bow. Then the room decides what to do next -- and they will.


GUEST OF HONOR THROUGHLINE
---------------------------

Introduce early: "Tonight is [NAME]'s evening. Everything that happens here tonight --
I want it to be for [NAME]."

Involve them in at least one routine -- ideally the book test or ultimate vacation,
where their choice drives the effect.

Return at the end: the last moment before the bow should reference them by name.
"[NAME] -- I hope tonight was everything you wanted it to be."

This makes the whole show feel like it was designed specifically for this event,
not just performed at it.
