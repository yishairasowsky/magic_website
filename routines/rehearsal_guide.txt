REHEARSAL GUIDE
===============
Based on Ben Cohen's advice


WHAT THE OUTLINE IS FOR
-----------------------
The bullet outline is not a script to memorize word for word.
It is a map of beats you must hit, in order, every time.
The words between the beats can change. The beats cannot.

Your job in rehearsal is to internalize the beats so deeply that
you could hit them in any order, in any words, under any circumstances --
and still land the effect.


HOW TO REHEARSE WITHOUT SPECTATORS
------------------------------------
Run the routine out loud, alone, standing up, as if the spectator is there.

For each beat:
  - Say the patter line in one way
  - Then say it in a different way
  - Then say it in a third way
  - Pick the version that feels most natural coming out of your mouth

Do this for every bullet. By the end, you'll have words that are yours --
not lines you memorized, but things you actually say.

For dead moments (while the spectator is writing, sealing, etc.):
  - Time them with a stopwatch
  - Practice filling exactly that amount of time with natural talk
  - The filler must sound like it just occurred to you -- not like you're killing time


THE MEANINGFUL vs RANDOM RULE  [Ben's most important note]
----------------------------------------------------------
Every piece of information you ask someone to think of must mean something to them.

BAD:  "Think of any number."
GOOD: "Think of a number that has meaning to you -- a birthday, a year, an age."

BAD:  "Think of any object."
GOOD: "Think of a specific object that matters to you -- something in your home,
       something you'd want to have with you if you had to leave quickly."

The difference: random information produces no emotional investment.
Meaningful information makes the spectator feel personally touched when you get it right.
That is what produces a genuine reaction.

Apply this to every routine. Go through each one and ask:
is what I'm asking them to think of meaningful -- or just random?


HOW TO BUILD A PREMISE
-----------------------
A premise is not an explanation. It is a story or a claim that makes the audience
curious and creates an expectation that what you're about to do is impossible.

You cannot say: "What I'm about to do will blow your mind."
You have to show them why it should be impossible -- through a story, a claim,
a phenomenon -- so that when you do it, they reach that conclusion themselves.

Structure:
  1. State a phenomenon or claim (true or not -- it doesn't matter, it's for the audience to decide)
  2. Make it personal or relatable -- something they can connect to
  3. End on the setup: "We're going to test that right now."

Under one minute. If you can't make it compelling in under a minute, cut it.


HOW TO KNOW IF THE PREMISE IS WORKING
--------------------------------------
By the time you finish your premise and begin the effect,
the room should be leaning forward slightly.

If they're leaning back -- the premise isn't working yet.
Go back and find a more relatable or more impossible-sounding version of the claim.


AFTER THE EFFECT
-----------------
Do not explain what just happened.
Do not ask for a reaction.
Do not dwell.

If they react -- let it happen, then move on.
If they don't -- move on anyway. After the show, figure out why the connection wasn't made.
Was the premise too weak? Was the information not meaningful enough?
Was the reveal not immediately visible to the whole room at once?

Those are the only three reasons an effect doesn't land.
